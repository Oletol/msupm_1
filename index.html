Two Traditions of Public Leadership

For more than a century, scholars of public administration have 0 drawn a distinction between two contrasting models of leadership. The first, usually described as bureaucratic, 1 emphasis on rules, written procedures and the impersonal authority of the office rather than of the individual who happens to occupy it. Officials working in this tradition are expected to be methodical, impartial and, above all, predictable. Critics argue that such organisations eventually become rigid, and that capable staff gradually lose the 2 to take the initiative.

The second model is commonly called charismatic. Here, authority 3 from the personal qualities of the leader — vision, energy and the ability to persuade. A charismatic mayor may 4 new life into a demoralised city council within a matter of months, and citizens frequently respond to such figures with a level of enthusiasm that no procedure could ever produce.

Yet this style carries obvious risks. When an entire institution 5 on one exceptional individual, it rarely develops the capacity to function without them. Historians of local government can 6 to dozens of towns that flourished under a remarkable leader and then declined sharply the moment that leader retired.

Recent research suggests that the most effective administrators do not 7 neatly into either category. They combine procedural discipline with genuine emotional awareness, and they are 8 of adjusting their behaviour to the demands of the situation rather than repeating a single style in every context.

Reading the Room: Emotional Intelligence in Public Relations

1When a government department announces an unwelcome decision — the closure of a small hospital, a change to pension rules, a new charge for parking in the city centre — attention naturally falls on the decision itself. Officials preparing such announcements tend to assume that any anger which follows is a reaction to the policy, and that the only realistic response is to explain the policy more thoroughly. A growing body of research into public communication suggests otherwise. Citizens are frequently just as offended by the manner of the announcement as by its content: by the impression that nobody in the department had paused to consider what the news would feel like to the people receiving it.

2The capacity to consider precisely that is what psychologists call emotional intelligence. The term entered academic literature in the 1990s and has since been divided into four broad abilities: perceiving emotion in oneself and in others, using emotion to guide thinking, understanding how emotions develop and change, and managing emotional responses. It is worth noting what the definition does not include. Emotional intelligence is not a synonym for warmth, and it is certainly not the same as being agreeable. An official who tells a room full of angry residents whatever they wish to hear has not demonstrated emotional intelligence; he has simply postponed the argument. The skill lies in reading a situation accurately and then choosing a response, which occasionally means delivering an unpopular message without pretending it is popular.

3The difference this makes can be surprisingly concrete. In a study of flood-response meetings in northern England, researchers compared recordings of officials addressing residents whose homes had been damaged. One spokesperson opened with the technical detail: drainage capacity, insurance procedures, the timetable for repairs. All of it was accurate, and almost none of it was heard. A second spokesperson, facing an equally hostile audience, began by naming the emotion in the room — she said that if she had spent three nights in a sports hall she would be furious too — and only afterwards moved on to the drainage figures. The information she conveyed was identical. The evaluations residents completed afterwards were not: the second meeting produced markedly higher ratings for competence, not merely for sympathy. Feeling understood, it seems, makes people willing to listen to detail they would otherwise dismiss.

4None of this comes free, however. The effort of managing one's own feelings while attending closely to everybody else's has a name in the research literature — emotional labour — and there is now substantial evidence that it exhausts the people who perform it. Front-line staff in housing offices, benefits centres and hospital reception areas report levels of fatigue that have little to do with the number of hours they work. What tires them is the requirement to appear calm and interested for eight hours, whatever they happen to feel. One researcher has described it as an expensive kind of acting, and the phrase is a useful corrective to the cheerful tone of much management training, which presents emotional intelligence as a resource that can simply be switched on. Departments that demand it without adjusting workloads are, in practice, transferring a cost from the institution to the individual.

5Whether the skill can be taught at all remains contested. Short courses certainly improve how participants score on questionnaires, but questionnaires measure what people believe about themselves rather than how they behave under pressure at half past four on a difficult afternoon. The more convincing results come from long-term programmes in which staff are observed, filmed and given detailed feedback over months — an approach few public bodies are willing to fund. My own view is that the evidence supports cautious optimism rather than enthusiasm: the ability appears to develop, but slowly, and only where senior managers model it themselves. A one-day workshop delivered to employees whose director never leaves his office is unlikely to change very much.

6For those responsible for recruitment, the practical consequence is uncomfortable. Selection procedures in the public sector are built around qualifications, written tests and formal interviews, all of which are relatively straightforward to defend if a candidate complains. Emotional intelligence resists that kind of documentation. Yet the officials whom citizens remember, and trust, are rarely the ones with the most impressive paper record. They are the ones who noticed, in a crowded room on a bad afternoon, what everybody else was feeling — and adjusted.

Past habits & states
  • used to + infinitive — repeated actions and past states: My father used to be a magistrate.
  • would + infinitive — repeated actions only, never states: He would arrive at seven every morning. (NOT would be)
  • be in the habit of + -ing — a more formal alternative.
  • be/get used to + -ing — be accustomed to something. A completely different structure.
Wishes & regrets
  • wish / if only + past simple — a present situation: I wish I had more time.
  • wish / if only + past perfect — a regret about the past: I wish I had said nothing.
  • wish + would — annoyance at another person's behaviour: I wish they would answer my emails.
  • should have + past participle — criticism of a past decision.
Perfect tenses
  • have + past participle with never … before, the first time, so far, already.
  • have been + -ing with for and since when the activity continues.
  • It is the first time I have seen… = I have never seen … before.
Exam procedure
  • Identify the structure being tested before you write anything.
  • Count your words: contractions such as hadn't count as two.
  • Never change the key word — not even its form.
  • Check that the meaning is genuinely identical, not merely similar.

Merit, Background and the Modern State

The modern civil service was invented, at least in part, to solve a problem of character. Before the middle of the nineteenth century, posts in most European administrations were distributed through patronage: a minister appointed the sons of his friends, and the question of whether they could do the work was rarely decisive. The reforms that followed — competitive examinations, permanent appointments, promotion on record rather than on recommendation — were designed to replace personal connection with demonstrable ability. It was, in its time, a radical idea.

A century and a half later, the principle is universally accepted and the practice remains stubbornly complicated. Examinations reward the skills that examinations measure, and those skills are unevenly distributed. A candidate who grew up in a household where three languages were spoken, where books lined the walls and where somebody at the dinner table could explain what a ministry actually does, arrives at the test with an advantage that has nothing to do with innate ability. Studies of recruitment in several European administrations consistently find that senior officials come from a narrower range of backgrounds than the populations they serve.

Why should this matter, provided the officials are competent? Two arguments are usually offered. The first is a question of legitimacy: institutions that look nothing like the society they govern find it harder to be trusted, particularly by the communities that are least represented in them. The second is more practical. Administrators design services for people whose lives they have to imagine, and imagination is easier when experience overlaps. An official who has waited for a bus in the rain at six in the morning will design a bus timetable differently from one who never has.

None of this amounts to an argument against merit. Nobody seriously proposes appointing incompetent officials in the interests of variety. The more useful conclusion is that merit is harder to identify than it looks, and that the confidence with which a well-prepared candidate performs in an interview is not the same thing as the capacity to serve the public well for thirty years. Several administrations now assess candidates on situational judgement rather than knowledge alone, and a few have begun recruiting apprentices at eighteen rather than graduates at twenty-two. Whether these experiments succeed will not be clear for another generation.

Family Dynasties in State Governance

The belief that a talent for government can be passed 0 down from one generation to the next is far older than democracy itself. In hereditary monarchies, office moved automatically from parent to child. In modern states the process is considerably subtler, 29 it has certainly not disappeared.

Research carried out in several European parliaments suggests that a candidate 30 father or mother has previously held public office is significantly more likely to be elected than an equally qualified rival without such a background. The effect appears to be strongest in regional assemblies, 31 voters are more likely to recognise a local surname.

Defenders of these families argue that children who grow 32 surrounded by discussions of policy acquire a practical understanding of the state that no university course can supply. There is 33 doubt that such an upbringing brings genuine advantages: useful contacts, early confidence and a familiarity with procedure that others acquire only after years in post.

Critics, 34 the other hand, point out that none of these advantages has anything to do with merit. If two candidates are equally competent, the one 35 the well-known name will almost always be preferred, and the public interest is not obviously served by the result.

For this reason, a number of governments have introduced rules preventing senior officials 36 appointing close relatives to any position under their direct control. Such rules are easy to write and notoriously difficult to enforce.
