The People Behind the Desk

470 words · CEFR B1+ · read twice

1Every morning, thousands of people walk into government offices. They come to register a car, to ask about a pension, to collect a certificate or to complain about a broken road. Most of them do not think about politics at all. For them, the state is one person: the man or the woman behind the desk. If that person is helpful, people say the system works. If that person is rude, people say the whole country is badly run. That is a heavy responsibility for one office worker with a computer and a queue.

2So what kind of person does this job well? Researchers who study public administration usually name three qualities. The first is reliability – doing what you promised, on the day you promised it. The second is fairness – giving the same answer to two people who have the same problem. The third is patience, and, according to many officials, it is by far the hardest of the three.

3Fairness sounds simple, but it is not. Imagine that two women come to the same window on the same morning. Both of them want a permit. One is polite and has brought all her documents. The other is angry, shouts at the officer, and has forgotten one paper. It is completely natural to want to help the first woman more. A fair officer treats them in exactly the same way and gives the second woman the same twenty minutes as the first. “That is the moment when you find out what kind of official you really are,” says one department head.

4Marina Petrova has worked in a housing department for eleven years. She explains the same rule about twenty times a day. “When I started, I used to get angry,” she says. “I thought: why don’t they read the website? Now I understand that people are not stupid. They are worried, and a person who is worried does not read carefully.” Her advice to new officers is short: listen first, explain second, and never begin a sentence with “you should have…”.

5But personality is not enough. Good systems matter too. Clear rules protect the officer as well as the citizen, because they make it possible to say no without saying “I don’t like you”. Training matters. So does a manager who behaves the way he expects his staff to behave. An honest person in a dishonest office usually leaves within two years.

6So where do these qualities come from? Many officials say the same thing: from home. They remember a grandmother who returned extra change to a shop, or a father who never took a day off work. Others learned it much later, from a colleague or from a first boss. Perhaps the real question is not where honesty comes from, but whether an institution is able to keep it alive.

Reading the Room: Emotional Intelligence in Public Relations

1When a government department announces an unwelcome decision – the closure of a small hospital, a change to pension rules, a new charge for parking in the city centre – attention naturally falls on the decision itself. Officials preparing such announcements tend to assume that any anger which follows is a reaction to the policy, and that the only realistic response is to explain the policy more thoroughly. A growing body of research into public communication suggests otherwise. Citizens are frequently just as offended by the manner of the announcement as by its content: by the impression that nobody in the department had paused to consider what the news would feel like to the people receiving it.

2The capacity to consider precisely that is what psychologists call emotional intelligence. The term entered academic literature in the 1990s and has since been divided into four broad abilities: perceiving emotion in oneself and in others, using emotion to guide thinking, understanding how emotions develop and change, and managing emotional responses. It is worth noting what the definition does not include. Emotional intelligence is not a synonym for warmth, and it is certainly not the same as being agreeable. An official who tells a room full of angry residents whatever they wish to hear has not demonstrated emotional intelligence; he has simply postponed the argument. The skill lies in reading a situation accurately and then choosing a response, which occasionally means delivering an unpopular message without pretending it is popular.

3The difference this makes can be surprisingly concrete. In a study of flood-response meetings in northern England, researchers compared recordings of officials addressing residents whose homes had been damaged. One spokesperson opened with the technical detail: drainage capacity, insurance procedures, the timetable for repairs. All of it was accurate, and almost none of it was heard. A second spokesperson, facing an equally hostile audience, began by naming the emotion in the room – she said that if she had spent three nights in a sports hall she would be furious too – and only afterwards moved on to the drainage figures. The information she conveyed was identical. The evaluations residents completed afterwards were not: the second meeting produced markedly higher ratings for competence, not merely for sympathy. Feeling understood, it seems, makes people willing to listen to detail they would otherwise dismiss.

4None of this comes free, however. The effort of managing one's own feelings while attending closely to everybody else's has a name in the research literature – emotional labour – and there is now substantial evidence that it exhausts the people who perform it. Front-line staff in housing offices, benefits centres and hospital reception areas report levels of fatigue that have little to do with the number of hours they work. What tires them is the requirement to appear calm and interested for eight hours, whatever they happen to feel. One researcher has described it as an expensive kind of acting, and the phrase is a useful corrective to the cheerful tone of much management training, which presents emotional intelligence as a resource that can simply be switched on. Departments that demand it without adjusting workloads are, in practice, transferring a cost from the institution to the individual.

5Whether the skill can be taught at all remains contested. Short courses certainly improve how participants score on questionnaires, but questionnaires measure what people believe about themselves rather than how they behave under pressure at half past four on a difficult afternoon. The more convincing results come from long-term programmes in which staff are observed, filmed and given detailed feedback over months – an approach few public bodies are willing to fund. My own view is that the evidence supports cautious optimism rather than enthusiasm: the ability appears to develop, but slowly, and only where senior managers model it themselves. A one-day workshop delivered to employees whose director never leaves his office is unlikely to change very much.

6For those responsible for recruitment, the practical consequence is uncomfortable. Selection procedures in the public sector are built around qualifications, written tests and formal interviews, all of which are relatively straightforward to defend if a candidate complains. Emotional intelligence resists that kind of documentation. Yet the officials whom citizens remember, and trust, are rarely the ones with the most impressive paper record. They are the ones who noticed, in a crowded room on a bad afternoon, what everybody else was feeling – and adjusted.

RuleAdverbs of frequency – where to put them

always · usually · often · sometimes · rarely · seldom · hardly ever · never

Writing

We do not start with a whole essay. We start with one paragraph – and with its first sentence.

40–50 minWritingB1+
Why we begin here. A piece of writing is not a chain of sentences; it is a chain of paragraphs, and every paragraph has a shape. If you can build one good paragraph, you can build a letter, a report and an essay. If you cannot, no amount of vocabulary will help you.
RuleThe shape of a paragraph

One paragraph = one idea. It normally has three parts:

  • Topic sentence – usually the first sentence. It says what the whole paragraph is about. It is a general statement, not a detail.
  • Supporting sentences – two to four sentences that explain, give reasons, or give examples.
  • Concluding sentence – optional; it closes the idea or leads to the next paragraph.
Patience is the quality that officials find hardest to keep. An officer at an information desk may explain the same rule twenty times a day. Every time, the person in front of the desk is hearing it for the first time. After eight hours, staying calm is no longer a feeling – it is a skill.

Test for a topic sentence: can the rest of the paragraph be predicted from it? If yes, it is a topic sentence. If it is too narrow (a date, a name, a number), it is a detail.

RulePunctuation – five rules that solve most problems
  • Capital letters: the first word of a sentence, the pronoun I, names of people, cities, countries, nationalities, languages, months, days, and official titles: On Monday, Mr Ivanov met the Minister of Education in Kazan.

Family Dynasties in State Governance

The belief that a talent for government can be passed 0 down from one generation to the next is far older than democracy itself. In hereditary monarchies, office moved automatically from parent to child. In modern states the process is considerably subtler, 29 it has certainly not disappeared.

Research carried out in several European parliaments suggests that a candidate 30 father or mother has previously held public office is significantly more likely to be elected than an equally qualified rival without such a background. The effect appears to be strongest in regional assemblies, 31 voters are more likely to recognise a local surname.

Defenders of these families argue that children who grow 32 surrounded by discussions of policy acquire a practical understanding of the state that no university course can supply. There is 33 doubt that such an upbringing brings genuine advantages: useful contacts, early confidence and a familiarity with procedure that others acquire only after years in post.

Critics, 34 the other hand, point out that none of these advantages has anything to do with merit. If two candidates are equally competent, the one 35 the well-known name will almost always be preferred, and the public interest is not obviously served by the result.

Before the City Wakes Up

520 words · CEFR B1+

1At half past four in the morning a city looks empty, but it is not. The first buses are already leaving the depot. Street cleaners are finishing the central square before anybody sees them. In a small brick building near the river, a woman on the night shift is watching the pressure in the water pumping station and writing numbers in a book, as somebody has done every night for forty years. None of these people will be thanked today. If they all do their work correctly, nobody will even notice that they exist.

2Most citizens believe that they meet the state only two or three times a year – when a passport expires, when a child starts school, when a certificate is needed. In reality they meet it before breakfast. The bus timetable, the waste collection, the traffic lights, the water in the tap, the pavement that is not covered in ice: every one of these is the result of an administrative decision taken by somebody in an office. Public administration is invisible when it works, and extremely visible when it does not.

3Sergey Bortnik, forty-one, works in a regional transport department. He arrives at twenty to eight, before the official start of the day, because the first hour is the only quiet one. He opens the complaints system and reads what came in overnight: a broken shelter, a bus that did not arrive, a driver who did not stop. At nine there is a short meeting. By eleven he is usually answering the phone, and by three he is writing the documents he did not manage to write in the morning. He says the job has one rule: do the boring things first, because the interesting ones will find you anyway.

4When researchers ask residents what annoys them most about public services, the answer is almost never money. It is time. People accept that a decision may be negative; what they cannot accept is waiting three weeks to be told. In one regional study, satisfaction rose sharply when the response time fell from twenty days to seven – even though the decisions themselves did not change at all.

5This is why service centres and online services have changed daily life so much. Fifteen years ago, renewing one document meant visiting three buildings, standing in a queue in each of them and taking a day off work. Today, in many regions, it means filling in a form on a phone and collecting the document once. The routine of officials has changed as well: less walking between offices with paper, more sitting in front of a screen, and a very different kind of tiredness at the end of the day.

6Something, however, has been lost. When an application arrives as a file, the officer no longer sees the person who sent it – the old man who does not understand the form, the mother with two children who has been waiting since eight. Efficiency is easy to measure. Whatever it is that disappears with the queue is not. Is that a price worth paying?

RulePunctuation – apostrophes and direct speech
  • Contractions: the apostrophe replaces the missing letters: I’m, don’t, it’s (= it is), we’re, can’t, wasn’t.
  • Possession: one owner takes ’s (the officer’s desk); more than one owner takes s’ (the officers’ desks); an irregular plural takes ’s (the children’s playground).

  • Never use an apostrophe to make a plural: three forms, NOT three form’s.
  • Direct speech: comma before the quotation, capital letter at the start of it, final punctuation inside the marks: She said, “Listen first, explain second.”   “Do you have proof of address?” he asked.
